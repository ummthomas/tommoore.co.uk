The brief

Challenge
Designing, testing and monetising APIs meant juggling multiple platforms. Developers faced a clunky development process and a slow time to value.
Objective
Standardise and simplify the API development workflow at every stage, so developers and organisations deliver faster and code more efficiently.

My role

I owned the developer tools experience at Rapid, from conception to release. I worked with product and engineering squads across three time zones to make the tools that developers use every day simpler and more effective.

Two very different users

Most of Rapid’s paying users weren’t technical, but a small group of experts drove half the revenue.

Understanding the existing product architecture

API development covers a vast technical landscape with a steep learning curve, especially for people without a computer science background. Rapid’s tools already covered most of the workflow, but the architecture grouped features by what they were rather than what users were trying to do.

We ran remote tests with each user group and I turned the findings into empathy maps. They showed that moving between tasks in a journey was the hardest part. That led to our central hypothesis.

Empathy map using data sourced from initial user research and testing sessions.

Core design principles

Four principles guided every decision, and each one shaped a specific part of the product.

Onboarding and shared understanding
Introduce core concepts and terms early, so users can navigate the product with confidence.
Guided progression
Support users from start to finish with clear steps, reducing friction and uncertainty at each stage.
Clarity over complexity
Use simple, familiar patterns and plain language to make functionality accessible without technical knowledge.
Adaptive experience
Offer depth for experts while keeping the core experience approachable for everyone else.

Making APIs accessible to everyone

Onboarding and shared understanding. Curated API discovery reduced the need for prior knowledge, while search-led entry supported people who already knew what they wanted.

To meet developers where they work, the hub also had to live inside their IDE. Designing for Visual Studio Code meant balancing powerful features against an already busy interface, while respecting each user’s theme and interaction habits.

The Rapid API Hub seamlessly integrating with Visual Studio Code, enabling users to search for and request from APIs without having to leave their developer environment.

Guided progression. Step-by-step flows reduced uncertainty, and pre-configured requests sped up setup, so users could get from finding an API to making their first call without getting stuck.

Clarity over complexity. Standardised endpoints built familiarity across APIs, and building requests inside the product simplified setup.

Iterating on a live product

The empathy maps gave me what I needed to make the case to business leaders for redesigning the architecture, in staged releases that fitted a lean, agile way of working.

They also helped other product managers prioritise their own work. We fixed small issues in existing workflows while planning the bigger changes, so Engineering could keep shipping while Product and Design worked out the best path.

Handover documentation example for a micro issues highlighted via testing. Processes to create design such as this would only take a matter of days due to using existing design system components.

Flow documentation example for a macro issue used to align stakeholders and provide deeper level analysis to inform further strategic planning, ideation and engineering focus.

Squad coordination across three geographies

Our team was spread across San Francisco, Central Europe and Tel Aviv. Working well together took careful scheduling, the right collaboration tools and clear communication habits.

Working across so many perspectives and cultures made me more adaptable, and taught me to be deliberate about how and when a distributed team makes decisions.

All of the team in a single timezone at our 2023 annual kick off in Marrakech.

Outcomes

After a year of iterative design, conversion rose 30% across every point in the workflow, from onboarding to monetisation, and across both core user segments.

Our IDE extensions had the highest daily active users of any of our platforms, reaching 100k users in their first week. In the Visual Studio Code marketplace, Rapid’s extension became the most popular that month.

Learnings

Be lean, and prioritise impact

Limited resources meant a short discovery period, so I focused on hypotheses with the biggest impact for the business. Accepting that I couldn’t master every technical detail at once helped me focus where design could add the most value.

Balance urgency with rigour

Urgency and rigour were both core values at Rapid. Knowing when to move fast and when to slow down was what let us keep delivering value without cutting corners.

What I’d do differently

Get closer to a complex domain sooner

More dogfooding and more time with technical peers would have shortened my ramp-up.

Share design patterns across the team

Docs, dashboard and SDK examples each grew their own conventions. A shared pattern library would have made the product feel coherent sooner.

Align business units across regions

Regional teams served customers with different levels of API maturity. Closer collaboration would have caught more edge cases earlier.

Tighten feedback loops with developers

Usability issues often reached us through support tickets. More frequent sessions with real customers would have caught friction sooner.
